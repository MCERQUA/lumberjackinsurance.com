24:T199a,<p>Driving in Northern Arizona is not like driving in Phoenix. Down the hill, the worst you'll deal with is heat and traffic. Up here at 7,000 feet, you've got snow-packed roads, black ice before dawn, the white-knuckle climb up I-17, and a very real chance that a 700-pound elk steps into your headlights on US-180 some evening in November.</p>
<p>Our roads ask more of your car and more of your insurance. Let's break down what Flagstaff-area drivers actually need so you're not caught short after a winter slide or an elk strike.</p>
<h2>Winter Driving on I-17, US-180, and Lake Mary Road</h2>
<p>If you commute or travel around Flagstaff, you know the trouble spots. The I-17 grade between Flagstaff and the Valley is notorious — it can be dry and 60 degrees in Camp Verde and a snowstorm at the top. US-180 toward the Snowbowl turns into a parade of out-of-towners with no snow experience every winter weekend. Lake Mary Road is dark, often icy, and full of wildlife.</p>
<p>Two hazards dominate up here:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Snow and ice.</strong> Heavy snowfall, refreeze overnight, and slush that turns to glass when the temperature drops. Flagstaff is genuinely one of the snowiest cities in the country.</li>
<li><strong>Black ice.</strong> The sneaky one. Bridges, shaded curves, and that early-morning stretch of Lake Mary Road can be invisible-slick when everything else looks fine.</li>
</ul>
<p>Good tires, slowing down, and leaving room are your first defense. But even careful drivers slide, get rear-ended, or slip off into a snowbank. That's where your auto policy earns its keep.</p>
<h2>Elk and Deer: Comprehensive vs. Collision</h2>
<p>Here's the question we get more than any other from Flagstaff drivers: <em>if I hit an elk, which coverage pays?</em></p>
<p>The answer surprises people. <strong>Hitting an animal is covered under comprehensive, not collision.</strong> Even though it feels like a collision, insurance treats an animal strike as a comprehensive (or "other than collision") loss — the same category as theft, hail, fire, and a cracked windshield.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Comprehensive</strong> pays when you <em>hit</em> an animal — an elk on US-180, a deer crossing Lake Mary Road, a stray cow on a forest road.</li>
<li><strong>Collision</strong> pays if you <em>swerve to avoid</em> an animal and hit something else — a guardrail, a tree, another car. Veering off the road to miss an elk and striking a ponderosa is a collision claim.</li>
</ul>
<p>That distinction matters because the two coverages can have different deductibles, and many people carry one but not the other.</p>
<h2>Why Comprehensive Coverage Really Matters Here</h2>
<p>In elk country, comprehensive is not optional in any practical sense. Northern Arizona has a large, active elk population, and they move most at dawn and dusk — exactly when many of us are driving to and from work. An elk weighs as much as a small piano, and a strike can total a vehicle and seriously injure the people inside.</p>
<p>US-180, I-17 north of town, Lake Mary Road, and the routes around Munds Park and Mountainaire all see regular wildlife crossings. Watch for the warning signs — they're posted where collisions actually happen — and remember that where there's one elk, there are usually more.</p>
<p>If you only carry liability, an elk strike comes entirely out of your pocket. Comprehensive coverage means a manageable deductible instead of a totaled car you still owe money on. It also covers the windshield cracked by gravel on a forest road, the hail dent from a monsoon storm, and the tree branch that falls in a winter snow load.</p>
<p>For most Flagstaff drivers, the modest cost of full coverage is well worth it.</p>
<h2>What to Do After a Winter Wreck or Animal Strike</h2>
<p>When the worst happens, a clear head and a few steps protect both your safety and your claim:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Get to safety.</strong> Pull off the road if you can, turn on hazards, and watch for traffic — secondary crashes on icy roads are common.</li>
<li><strong>Check for injuries</strong> and call 911 if anyone is hurt or if you've hit a large animal blocking the road.</li>
<li><strong>Document everything.</strong> Photos of the vehicles, the animal if present, road conditions, and the surrounding area. Snow, ice, and skid marks tell the story.</li>
<li><strong>Exchange information</strong> with any other drivers, and get a police or incident report number — especially helpful for elk strikes and single-vehicle slides.</li>
<li><strong>Don't admit fault</strong> at the scene. Stick to the facts and let the adjusters sort it out.</li>
<li><strong>Call your agent or carrier</strong> promptly to start the claim while details are fresh.</li>
</ol>
<p>For an animal strike, the report and your photos help confirm it's a comprehensive claim. For a winter slide, conditions matter, so note the weather, the time, and the exact spot.</p>
<h2>Coverage Tips for Flagstaff Drivers</h2>
<p>A few adjustments make a real difference for Northern Arizona conditions:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Carry comprehensive and collision,</strong> not just liability — especially with wildlife and snow in the mix.</li>
<li><strong>Pick deductibles you can actually afford</strong> to pay in a single bad week. A low comprehensive deductible stings less after an elk strike.</li>
<li><strong>Add roadside assistance and towing.</strong> A breakdown or slide-off on a remote stretch of Lake Mary Road in winter is no time to be stranded.</li>
<li><strong>Consider rental reimbursement.</strong> If your car is in the shop after an elk strike, this keeps you moving.</li>
<li><strong>Make sure your limits are adequate.</strong> A serious multi-car winter pileup on I-17 can generate big liability claims — don't carry bare-minimum limits.</li>
<li><strong>Glass coverage</strong> is cheap and handy given how much gravel and ice our roads throw at windshields.</li>
</ul>
